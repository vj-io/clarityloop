Why we built it

On sites where supervisors and workers speak different languages, everyday talk is slow and instructions get misunderstood. A review of 60 studies found language barriers to be the most dominant safety risk factor for migrant construction workers (Guan et al., ECAM, 2022). A study of construction workers in Chennai traced most injuries in its sample to basic language barriers and recommended instructions in workers' own language (Meeral et al., Cureus, 2023). In healthcare, teach-back — the listener repeats an instruction in their own words — is a recommended way to confirm understanding (Yen & Leasure, 2019). We cite these as context, not as proof that the app works.

Existing work-chat apps translate messages and show read receipts. A read receipt shows that a message was opened, not that it was understood. Nothing checks, instruction by instruction, that the worker's understanding matches what the supervisor meant.

What ClarityLoop does

Layer 1 · Chat in your own language

Each person reads and writes in English, Hindi (including Hinglish typed in Roman letters) or Tamil. Every received message is shown in the reader's language; "See original" shows the exact words sent. Place names, numbers and times are protected.

Layer 2 · Meaning check

The supervisor sends a task: action, object, place, time, quantity. There is no OK button. The worker says it back in their own words. A card compares each field side by side and blocks Confirm while anything differs or is unclear.

At crew scale

One task can go to the whole crew: every worker gets it in their own language and says it back separately. A live roll-call board in the crew chat shows who understood; only the supervisor sees which field a worker got wrong, and can press Confirm on the row. Nobody is shown up in front of the crew.

Safety briefing check

The supervisor picks 2–5 points from the crew's safety list (or a preset such as work at height). Each worker says them back in one reply. AI maps the words onto the list; code checks every picked point was said. The roll-call shows what is not said yet, and a printable record is kept. An extra check, not a guarantee.

Also built: voice input (browser speech recognition; the words land in the box to read first) and read-aloud; site words, a crew glossary used in translation that the supervisor approves; Make this a task from a chat message (AI pre-fills the form, the supervisor still sends); Remind for a worker who has not said a task back (supervisor-triggered, at most once per 10 minutes); Send again chips for recent tasks; an insights page for tasks and safety briefings, with a CSV download; and printable task and briefing records. Site words also steer how a task's object is translated. Messages typed offline are queued and sent once.

The translation was fine and the meaning still slipped. They sort it out in the same chat, the worker restates, the card turns green, both confirm, and an Understood card records it.

What stops someone clicking past the check. The worker has no OK button. The server refuses Confirm until every field matches or was accepted, and both people must confirm. A red mismatch can never be accepted. The supervisor can accept an unclear field only when the worker gave a value for it: after a bare "OK sir", or a reply the AI could not read, there is nothing to accept and the worker must say it back again. A check that passed through acceptances is titled "Match, with fields the supervisor accepted" in the record. It does not stop a worker who repeats the right words without understanding them.

How it is built

  • Next.js (App Router) on Vercel; Supabase Auth and Postgres with row-level security on every table; Nexconn for live conversations.
  • AI: four narrow jobs (google/gemini-3.5-flash-lite, with a fallback chain of other models when one is out of quota): translate text; make alternative names for a task's object; read a worker's reply into fixed fields (also used to pre-fill "Make this a task"); say which listed safety points a reply mentioned. Outputs are checked with zod and code.
  • The AI never decides. The AI reader never sees the supervisor's values; plain code compares the fields and the safety points (work-hours rule: a bare hour 1–6 is read as afternoon and shown, so either person can correct it). An unreadable reply gives "could not check", not a guess. Only people confirm.
  • Record keeping. Tasks, restatements, checks and state changes are stored with who and when; Nexconn is never the only copy.
  • Security and privacy. Secrets are server-side only. Workers join with a crew code and a hashed 4-digit PIN; tries are counted atomically (5, then a 5-minute lock). Translation is limited to conversation members, and the routes that call the AI for chat, tasks and say-backs have per-minute limits. Only message text and the crew's lists go to the AI. No location, no photos of people, no attendance.

What we know is not done

  • No pilot with real workers has run.
  • No native speaker has rated the translations; the test sets are small and written by us.
  • The translation speed target (median under 1.5 s) is not met on the current free-tier model key.

Where it goes next

Not built yet. Voice messages, phone-number sign-in, scheduled reminders, photos, a shift-handover check, a dashboard across crews and multi-site admin, audit export beyond the task CSV and printable pages, more languages, SMS and WhatsApp reach, single sign-on, API and webhooks, billing, native apps. Next step: a pilot with one real crew.

Business (a plan to test). The employer pays, workers use it free: per active worker per month, with a free tier for one small crew. First buyers: mid-size contractors, facility-management firms, warehouses. What compounds: crew glossaries, misunderstanding data, and a trusted record of what was agreed.
